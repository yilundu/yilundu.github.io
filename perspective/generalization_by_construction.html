Generalizable intelligence

Generalization by Construction

What learning should leave open for inference

August 29, 2026

The ultimate goal of artificial intelligence is to build systems that can act effectively in situations they have never seen before. This is especially important in robotics, where a robot will face new environments, objects, constraints, and goals. The dominant approach, however, is to collect ever-larger datasets and train end-to-end neural networks to map observations directly to behavior. This has produced powerful systems, but it is practically impossible to gather examples covering the open-ended space of possible situations.

One important direction in symbolic AI began with a different premise. It separated knowledge from the procedure used to reason with it. Facts, relations, rules, actions, and goals were represented as explicit objects that could be reused and recombined after a goal was specified. A planner could then pursue a new goal or incorporate a new constraint without training a new model. McCarthy's Programs with Common Sense and the STRIPS planning system are early examples of this approach.

The difficulty was deciding what to represent and how to search over it. People often had to choose the symbols, predicates, and operators, connect perception to them, and decide which details of the world to ignore. Search became difficult in large, continuous, and partially observed spaces. For images, video, language, and physical interaction, even the right vocabulary is difficult to specify in advance.

Deep learning made it possible to learn powerful representations directly from large, high-dimensional datasets. When a network is trained to predict behavior directly, the representations and the procedure for using them are learned together. The resulting mapping can generalize to situations that follow the regularities it has learned. But when a new problem falls outside those regularities, the system may have no explicit way to compare alternatives or revise an intermediate decision.

I argue that a globally unfamiliar problem can often be solved by recombining locally familiar structure. A robot may face a new situation even when the relevant objects, dynamics, and skills are all familiar. By learning models of this reusable structure, we can give the robot the pieces needed to construct a solution after the task is known. Inference then determines how to combine these pieces and searches for a solution that satisfies the current goals and constraints. The central design question is what learning should capture in advance and what it should leave open for inference to construct.

Core claim: Learning captures locally reusable structure. Inference uses it to construct solutions to globally unfamiliar problems.

The thesis

Drawing the amortization boundary

Every system divides problem solving between choices absorbed into reusable computation before the current problem is known and choices resolved through inference for the problem at hand. I call the placement of this division the amortization boundary. Training can amortize choices that recur across problems, including choices whose dependence on the input can be learned reliably. Choices that a future problem may need to reconsider must instead remain open to inference. Here I use inference to mean problem-specific search, selection, or refinement over choices that remain open to revision for the current problem.

The boundary trades computation for flexibility. If a recurring decision remains open even though the current problem provides no new information about it, inference wastes work. If nearly every decision is fixed in advance, a new goal or constraint may have nowhere to enter. The aim is therefore not to maximize inference, but to leave open the choices whose values cannot safely be determined in advance.

The amortization boundary also changes the generalization problem itself. A direct predictor must learn how a problem maps to a complete solution. Solution inference instead uses a learned criterion for judging candidates, while search constructs a solution for the current problem. Once a candidate is proposed, judging it can depend on fewer aspects of the problem and require a simpler computation than producing the solution directly. When this judgment depends on simpler, reusable structure, learning the criterion can support better generalization than learning the full problem-to-solution mapping.

Model composition extends this idea to the criterion itself. A learned factor can judge whether a candidate satisfies one requirement, such as stable support or clearance from nearby objects. Each factor need only represent the relationships relevant to that requirement. By composing these factors, the system can construct a model for a new combination of requirements without learning a separate model for the entire task. I expect this separation to be especially useful when goals and constraints change while the local relationships and dynamics needed to evaluate candidates remain reusable.

Program specialization offers a useful analogy. A programmer can compile known assumptions into an efficient executable, while retaining a higher-level program in which those assumptions can be revised. A direct neural policy resembles an aggressively specialized executable. It is fast to run, but hard to adapt when a new problem violates its compiled assumptions. An energy function or world model can preserve a higher-level description of possible solutions or futures that sampling, optimization, or planning specializes for the current problem.

The representation also determines how much of the problem must be resolved through search. A planner can search over task-relevant states, subgoals, or trajectories while learned skills handle familiar local transitions. The planner can then construct a new course of action without searching over every motor command.

This suggests four practical steps.

  1. Identify a decision whose answer changes across tasks while the structure needed to evaluate alternatives remains reusable.
  2. Choose a level of representation at which that choice can be inspected and revised.
  3. Learn reusable structure that helps resolve the choice without fixing it in advance.
  4. Use inference to resolve it for the current problem.

My PhD thesis and Compositional Generative Modeling developed this approach through the composition of learned energy landscapes. Here I use the amortization boundary to examine how the same principle can shape both the solutions a system constructs and the computation it uses to construct them.

Hierarchical inference

Consider a household robot asked to bring a mug of coffee from a crowded kitchen counter to someone at the table. It must lift the mug without disturbing the surrounding dishes, carry it without spilling, and set it within easy reach. The robot may have encountered every object and practiced every skill involved without having faced this particular combination.

I distinguish five scopes by what inference can reconfigure. The first three construct the problem and its solution, while the final two construct the computation used to solve it.

Model composition combines the goals, constraints, and dynamics that guide this search. Procedure control decides how the search proceeds, while reasoner organization determines which specialists contribute and how they communicate.

Solution inference

Search a learned solution space

Deferred decisionWhich candidate?

In solution inference, learning provides an evaluator that scores proposed solutions. Given the current problem, a sampler or optimizer uses this evaluator to compare candidates and revise them toward a better solution. The evaluator remains fixed during this search, while the candidate changes.

In the coffee task, the robot can revise a placement that is unstable or difficult for the person to reach. A direct predictor must produce a suitable placement from the kitchen scene. Given a proposed placement, an evaluator can judge familiar properties such as stability and reachability without learning how to produce the placement itself. Search uses these judgments to construct a placement for the current scene.

An energy-based model makes this separation explicit by assigning lower energy to preferred candidates. A search procedure such as Langevin dynamics follows the negative energy gradient while adding random noise. The learned landscape can be searched from different starting points and for different numbers of steps. A frozen language model can also supply an evaluator, with iterative sampling targeting a sharpened power distribution derived from its sequence likelihood.

The potential advantage is clearest when evaluating a candidate requires a simpler computation than producing it. In shortest-path prediction through energy minimization, a model trained on ten-node graphs achieves lower error on fifteen-node graphs than the feedforward and iterative baselines tested. The associated verifier–generator analysis gives a computational motivation for learning such a criterion.

Training should shape the landscape for the search procedure that will use it. A sequence of landscapes can guide inference from broad, smooth basins toward sharper constraints, helping the solver approach a solution before resolving its finer details. Search needs both a landscape that supports revision and scores that remain informative about solution quality as it explores new candidates.

Model composition

Compose the model required by the problem

Deferred decisionWhich task model?

A new task may require a combination of goals and constraints that no single learned model represents. Model composition lets the system construct this model after the task is known. Inference selects the relevant factors and combines them into a model that guides the search for a solution.

For the coffee task, the robot can combine factors that favor an upright mug, clearance from nearby dishes, stable support on the table, and reachability for the person. A support factor can evaluate the mug and its supporting surface, while a clearance factor evaluates the mug and nearby objects. Neither factor needs to model the entire kitchen arrangement. By composing these local judgments, the robot can evaluate an arrangement it has never seen as a whole. The factors support new combinations when their judgments remain valid in the new context and together capture the relationships needed to evaluate a solution.

Composing additional instances of a learned factor can let a model address larger problems than those used in training. For Boolean satisfiability, clause-level energies can be learned and their composition refined on small problems, then composed into larger instances at test time. With training instances of at most twenty variables, this approach solves more fifty-variable problems than the neural baselines tested.

Composition provides a way to program a new task using models learned from earlier data. This retains the flexibility of symbolic predicates and operators while allowing the factors to represent relations that are difficult to specify by hand. For a robot, a symbolic relation graph can select continuous factors and ground them into object poses. More broadly, models operating on different modalities can communicate through a shared candidate. A generator proposes a candidate, and independently trained scorers guide its revision. Their ability to work together depends on this common interface.

The vocabulary of factors need not be fixed by hand. Learning can discover factors without concept labels. It can also infer a new task factor from demonstrations while leaving a pretrained generator unchanged. A newly learned factor can then be combined with earlier ones to express further tasks.

These factors define a new inference problem, so the solver is part of the composition design. For example, standard reverse diffusion with composed scores does not in general sample the intended product distribution.

For decision making, composition brings together a description of what should happen and a model of what actions can cause. Objectives can combine goals, rewards, and constraints, while dynamics can combine models of objects, agents, and interactions. Action planning uses the resulting model to search for actions whose predicted consequences satisfy the objective.

Action planning

Plan through possible futures

Deferred decisionWhich actions reach the goal?

Once the robot has chosen a placement, it must find a way to carry the mug there without spilling or disturbing nearby dishes. Planning leaves the action sequence open for search. The planner predicts the consequences of possible actions and searches for a sequence expected to reach the desired state.

The dynamics of carrying the mug do not have to be learned again each time the person asks for it in a different place. A learned dynamics model predicts what an action will do, while the current goal determines which predicted consequences are desirable. Separating these two questions lets a planner construct new behavior under familiar dynamics. Behavior that recurs can later be absorbed into a policy or skill.

A planner can represent a possible future as intermediate states or as a complete state-action trajectory. Searching over intermediate states under learned dynamics allowed a planner to route around obstacles not encountered during training. A model of complete state-action trajectories provides a space of possible behaviors that new rewards, endpoints, or constraints can guide. In both cases, specifying a new task changes the search objective while reusing the learned model. In predictive control, the robot repeats this search as new observations arrive.

The representation also determines how much detail the planner must resolve at once. For longer-horizon tasks, a visual trajectory can describe how the scene should change while an embodiment-specific model determines the actions. Search over visual proposals can then construct longer plans from shorter predicted transitions. We can further compose language, video, and action models so that high-level steps are grounded in the scene and checked for physical executability.

A strong action proposal gives this search a useful starting point. In World Action Planner, a vision-language model proposes actions and an action-conditioned world model predicts their consequences. Global correction and local search revise the proposal for the current task. The proposal model therefore does not have to produce the final behavior by itself. Judging whether an imagined consequence makes progress can be easier than generating the entire successful behavior at once, giving planning the same potential advantage as solution inference.

Procedure control

Control the reasoning process

Deferred decisionWhich operation and for how long?

After a failed attempt, a system has to decide whether to start over, repair part of the candidate, or try a different operation. Procedure control makes this choice from the evidence accumulated during search. Generators, verifiers, and search procedures can be reused while the sequence of operations changes with the problem. Even with a fixed controller and total budget, the system can direct computation toward the parts of a solution that remain unresolved.

A failed rollout may still contain useful work. If a verifier can identify where a generated trajectory first goes wrong, the system can preserve a valid prefix and resume generation from it. The generate–verify–restart loop stays fixed, while verifier evidence determines which prefix to expand and when to stop. On Keys-and-Doors puzzles with solution paths longer than those in training, this procedure improves success over sampling complete rollouts at the same generation budget. Symbolic verification identifies the prefixes that can be retained, and every solved episode in this held-out setting comes from a restarted branch. Preserving intermediate progress makes it possible to solve puzzles that the model rarely solves in one complete rollout.

For the robot, a poor view of the mug may call for another perception step, while a shifted grasp may require checking the mug's orientation or repairing part of the plan. These failures call for different computations. The controller should preserve what remains useful and direct new work toward the decision that needs to change. It can also use the model's estimated likelihood of the current plan to decide whether to continue or replan from the existing trajectory.

How much computation to spend is also a decision about the current problem. A controller can adjust model size and precision to balance answer quality against delay, or stop once it has enough information to act. For an embodied system, the value of another reasoning step depends on whether it can improve the action before that action is needed.

Reasoner organization

Assemble the reasoning system

Deferred decisionWhich reasoners and connections?

If the robot encounters an unfamiliar, fragile mug, it may need to consult a grasping specialist and pass that judgment to the planner. Reasoner organization leaves this choice of participants open to inference, along with their roles, shared state, and connections.

I expect this ability to reorganize specialists to support generalization to tasks they were never trained to solve together. Each specialist can contribute a familiar capability even when the full task requires an unfamiliar combination. Systems that debate candidate answers or combine complementary verification judgments already use interaction within a fixed organization. Learning can also produce complementary generator and critic behaviors within such an organization. The further choice is whether the task itself determines which reasoners interact.

A task-dependent organization can emerge from local coordination rules. In Economy of Minds, auctions select a problem-dependent sequence of specialists without a central scheduler. The population stays fixed during evaluation, while economic selection reshapes it across optimization episodes. Selecting who acts also determines what computation runs next, so this mechanism spans procedure control and reasoner organization.

To test whether reorganization itself supports generalization, I would hold the specialists and computation budget fixed, then compare a task-dependent organization with a fixed one on new task families.

Integration test

Why embodiment is central

A robot has to make these different forms of inference work together while it acts. In the coffee task, a placement may be stable and within the person's reach yet impossible for the robot to reach safely. Planning must then reopen the placement choice. A failed grasp may require a new trajectory, while persistent uncertainty may call for a different procedure or specialist. Evidence from one scope can require the system to revise a decision made at another. These revisions must happen in time for the robot to act.

Conclusion

Generalization as construction

A problem can be globally unfamiliar even when the structure needed to solve it is locally familiar. Judging a candidate can be simpler than producing the whole solution, and local factors can remain useful across new combinations of goals and constraints. Learning can capture these reusable judgments while inference constructs the solution required by the current problem.

I expect general-purpose agents to build on learned models, skills, and specialists while constructing the plans and reasoning procedures required by each new task. As a solution recurs, learning can absorb the repeated parts of its construction. The amortization boundary can therefore move with experience. Learning should make familiar tasks faster to solve while preserving the ability to construct new solutions when the goal, the world, or the available expertise changes.
