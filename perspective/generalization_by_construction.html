Generalizable intelligence

Generalization by Construction

What learning should leave open for inference

August 29, 2026

The ultimate goal of artificial intelligence is to build systems that can act effectively in situations they have never seen before. This is especially important in robotics, where a robot will face new environments, objects, constraints, and goals. The dominant approach, however, is to collect ever-larger datasets and train end-to-end neural networks to map observations directly to behavior. This has produced powerful systems, but it is practically impossible to gather examples covering the open-ended space of possible situations.

One important direction in symbolic AI began with a different premise. It separated knowledge from the procedure used to reason with it. Facts, relations, rules, actions, and goals were represented as explicit objects that could be reused and recombined after a goal was specified. A planner could then pursue a new goal or incorporate a new constraint without training a new model. McCarthy's Programs with Common Sense and the STRIPS planning system are early examples of this approach.

The difficulty was in the representations and search procedures this approach assumed. In many such systems, people had to choose the symbols, predicates, and operators, connect perception to them, and then decide which details of the world to ignore. Search also became difficult in large, continuous, and partially observed spaces. These problems are especially severe for images, video, language, and physical interaction, where the right vocabulary is difficult to specify in advance.

Deep learning addressed a central limitation of symbolic AI by making powerful representations learnable directly from large, high-dimensional datasets. In its dominant end-to-end form, however, deep learning couples the learned representation to the procedure that produces an output. This absorbs most problem solving into a fixed mapping, making it difficult for the network to generalize to problems it was not explicitly trained to solve.

I argue that a globally unfamiliar problem can often be solved by recombining locally familiar structure. A robot may face a new situation even when the relevant objects, dynamics, and skills are all familiar. Learning should capture repeated structure without making every problem-dependent choice in advance. Inference should then use this learned structure to construct a solution through composition and search. Generalization depends not only on the amount of test-time computation, but on what learning leaves open for inference to revise.

In this perspective, learning leaves some problem-dependent choices explicit and unresolved. After the problem is known, inference constructs and may revise their values through comparison, search, refinement, or recombination. The object being constructed may be a candidate, a task model, a course of action, a sequence of reasoning operations, or an organization of reasoners.

Core claim: A system generalizes by learning reusable structure while leaving problem-dependent choices open to revision at test time.

The thesis

Drawing the amortization boundary

Every system divides problem solving between choices absorbed into reusable computation before the current problem is known and choices resolved through inference for the problem at hand. I call the placement of this division the amortization boundary. Training can amortize choices that recur across problems, including choices whose dependence on the input can be learned reliably. Choices that a future problem may need to reconsider must instead remain open to inference. Here I use inference to mean problem-specific search, selection, or refinement over choices left unresolved until the problem is known.

The boundary trades computation for flexibility. If a recurring decision remains open even though the current problem provides no new information about it, inference wastes work. If nearly every decision is fixed in advance, a new goal or constraint may have nowhere to enter. The aim is therefore not to maximize inference, but to leave open the choices whose values cannot safely be determined in advance.

A useful analogy is program specialization. Assumptions known in advance can be compiled into an efficient executable. When those assumptions change, however, the system must return to a higher-level program in which the relevant functions and variables remain explicit. A direct neural policy resembles an aggressively specialized executable. It is fast to run but difficult to adapt when a new problem violates its compiled assumptions. An energy function or world model can instead preserve a higher-level description of solutions or futures. Sampling, optimization, or planning then specializes that description for the current problem.

The amortization boundary also changes the generalization problem itself. A direct predictor must learn how a problem maps to a complete solution. Solution inference instead learns a reusable criterion for judging candidates, while search constructs a solution for the current problem. Once a candidate is proposed, judging it can depend on fewer aspects of the problem and require a simpler computation than producing the solution directly. This simpler criterion is therefore more likely to generalize to unfamiliar problems than the full problem-to-solution mapping a direct predictor must learn.

Model composition moves another choice to the inference side of the boundary. Rather than learning the criterion for each task as a whole, the system learns reusable factors for individual goals, constraints, or relationships. Because each factor depends on a narrower part of the problem, inference can recombine them to construct criteria for tasks not seen during training.

The boundary depends not only on which choices remain open, but also on their level of abstraction. A planner does not need to search over every motor command. It can instead search over task-relevant states, subgoals, or trajectories while learned skills handle familiar local transitions. A useful representation keeps problem-dependent choices explicit without reopening every familiar detail.

This suggests four practical steps.

  1. Identify the choice that depends on the current goal, constraint, or observation.
  2. Choose a level of representation at which that choice can be inspected and revised.
  3. Learn reusable structure that helps resolve the choice without fixing it in advance.
  4. Use inference to resolve it for the current problem.

My PhD thesis developed this view for planning and the composition of learned energy functions. The same amortization boundary applies to the procedure and organization of reasoning.

The framework

Hierarchical inference

I distinguish five scopes by what inference can reconfigure. At the narrowest, inference searches for a candidate under a fixed model. At broader scopes, inference may compose the task model, plan a course of action, control the reasoning procedure, or assemble the reasoning system itself. The first three construct the problem and its solution, while the final two extend inference to the computation that solves it. Across all five, learning captures what recurs while inference resolves problem-dependent choices.

These scopes form a dependency hierarchy, not a set of mutually exclusive levels or stages in a fixed pipeline. Broader scopes can alter the model or computation within which narrower inference runs, so a system may operate across several at once. Which scopes apply depends on the choices left open to inference, not on the kind of model being used. A world model, for example, supports solution inference when it is used to search among possible futures, model composition when inference combines it with a new goal or constraint, and action planning when inference searches over actions and their predicted consequences.

The final two scopes make a parallel distinction within computation. Procedure control chooses what an assembled system does next, including which solver or check to run and when to stop. Reasoner organization changes the assembled system itself by selecting the participants, assigning their roles, or routing information among them. Specialized modules alone do not imply reasoner organization. Some aspect of participation, roles, or connections must remain open to inference.

The hierarchy becomes especially concrete in robotics, where an agent must construct both a desired outcome and a way to reach it while each action changes the state on which the next decision depends.

Consider a household robot asked to bring a mug of coffee from a crowded kitchen counter to someone at the table. It must lift the mug without disturbing the surrounding dishes, carry it without spilling, and set it within easy reach. The robot may have encountered every object and practiced every skill involved without having faced this particular combination. Solution inference searches for a stable placement. Model composition combines support, uprightness, clearance, and reachability into the model that judges it. Action planning searches for actions whose consequences reach that outcome. Procedure control decides when to verify, repair, or act, while reasoner organization brings in a perception or grasping specialist when the current system lacks the required knowledge.

Solution inference

Search a learned solution space

Deferred decisionWhich candidate?

Solution inference keeps the learned model fixed while leaving the candidate open for search at test time. A direct predictor folds the criterion for a good answer into the same learned computation that produces it. An energy function or learned evaluator separates these roles by scoring proposed candidates, while a sampler or optimizer searches for one that scores well. The defining characteristic of solution inference is that candidates remain open to comparison and revision rather than being fixed by a single learned pass.

In the coffee task, the robot can revise a placement that is unstable or difficult for the person to reach rather than accept its first proposal. A direct predictor must learn how the entire kitchen scene maps to a suitable placement. A learned evaluator faces a narrower problem. Given a proposed placement, it can judge familiar properties such as stability and reachability without learning how to produce the placement itself. Because this judgment can depend on fewer aspects of the scene and require a simpler computation, the learned criterion is more likely to generalize to unfamiliar kitchen configurations than the full scene-to-placement mapping.

Energy-based models are a concrete implementation of this separation. In Implicit Generation and Generalization in Energy-Based Models, the learned object is a landscape rather than a fixed generator. Langevin dynamics, an iterative stochastic sampler, can search this landscape without imposing a fixed generation order. The sampler can also start from an external proposal or run for different numbers of steps. Learning Iterative Reasoning through Energy Minimization applies the same idea to reasoning by minimizing a conditional landscape. Its verifier–generator analysis examines when recognizing a valid solution can require a simpler model than constructing one.

The practical question is what geometry allows inference to revise candidates reliably. Learning Iterative Reasoning through Energy Diffusion moves through a sequence of landscapes, from broad, smooth basins toward sharper constraints. Reasoning with Sampling applies the same separation to a frozen language model, using a sharpened power distribution derived from its sequence likelihood as the target of an iterative sampler that can revisit earlier choices. Across both settings, additional inference is useful only when the learned geometry supports reliable revision.

Model composition

Compose the model required by the problem

Deferred decisionWhich task model?

Solution inference searches for a candidate under a fixed model. Model composition changes the model that defines this search. The current task determines which reusable factors should constrain the answer. Inference may select existing factors, compose them, or infer a new one from the current problem. What composition produces is not a full answer, but a problem-specific criterion or distribution that can guide solution inference. Rather than serving only as conditioning input to a monolithic predictor, a new task can determine which reusable factors are selected, grounded, and assembled into this task-specific model.

The task model used to judge candidate placements does not need to be learned as a whole for this exact kitchen scene. The robot can construct it by combining factors that favor an upright mug, clearance from nearby dishes, stable support on the table, and reachability for the person. The complete arrangement is globally unfamiliar, but each factor expresses locally familiar structure. Here, generalization comes from constructing a new global model from these reusable local judgments, instead of having one model extrapolate across the entire combination at once.

Symbolic systems obtained much of their flexibility by composing reusable predicates and operators. The aim is not to recover a particular symbolic vocabulary, but the operational separation these systems exploited. Reusable pieces remain addressable after the task is known, while their continuous semantics can be learned from data. The components do not need to share an architecture or modality, but they do need compatible interfaces. They must share an object of inference, provide compatible ways to evaluate or guide it, and support a procedure that reconciles their constraints.

Composition can operate across several kinds of learned structure. Concept distributions can be combined through conjunction, disjunction, and negation, named spatial relations can be assembled into a scene, and independently trained score models can be combined during denoising. Iterative consensus extends the same principle across architectures by letting a generator and independently trained vision, language, or task-specific scorers communicate through a shared candidate.

A compositional system must also learn the pieces it will later reuse. Unsupervised Learning of Compositional Energy Concepts discovers reusable factors without concept labels, while Few-Shot Task Learning through Inverse Generative Modeling infers a new task factor from demonstrations without changing the pretrained generator. Learning therefore does not determine the final task model in advance. It can instead produce a vocabulary of factors whose selection and combination remain open. Once learned, a new factor should compose with earlier factors without retraining them, enlarging the family of tasks the system can construct.

Composition must be grounded and matched to an inference procedure. Set It Up! uses a symbolic relation graph to select learned continuous factors and ground them into object poses. Reduce, Reuse, Recycle shows why the solver cannot be treated as an afterthought. Standard reverse diffusion does not realize the product distribution implied by composed scores. Generalizable Reasoning through Compositional Energy Minimization carries the same principle into reasoning, where local learned criteria become reusable verifiers inside a larger problem. The factors, their interface, and the procedure that reconciles them must therefore be designed together.

Decision making is a particularly important instance of model composition. A model of the desired future represents what outcomes matter, while action-conditioned dynamics represent what proposed interventions could cause. Both sides can be composed. Dynamics may combine reusable models of objects, agents, and interactions. Objectives may combine goals, rewards, and constraints. Model composition brings these pieces together to define the decision problem. Action planning then searches for interventions whose predicted consequences reach the desired outcome.

Action planning

Plan through possible futures

Deferred decisionWhich actions reach the goal?

Composing a desired outcome with a model of what can happen defines a decision problem, but it does not determine how the agent should act. In the coffee task, the robot must still choose a grasp, lift the mug clear of nearby dishes, carry it without spilling, and place it at the selected location. Planning leaves this action sequence open. The planner predicts the consequences of possible actions and searches for a sequence expected to reach the desired state.

A direct policy instead compiles the use of dynamics and objectives into a mapping from observations and goals to actions. This makes action selection efficient but commits in advance to how the agent will use its knowledge. Such a policy can generalize when a new situation follows regularities captured in the learned mapping. When the situation requires a combination not captured by that mapping, however, the policy exposes neither the dynamics nor a search process that can construct a new behavior. The distinction is therefore not policy versus planning as permanent alternatives, but which decisions should be compiled and which must remain open.

Planning does not always operate directly on individual motor commands. It can search over subgoals, intermediate states, or complete trajectories, depending on what must be decided for the current task. Model-Based Planning with Energy-Based Models inferred intermediate states under learned dynamics, allowing a planner to route around obstacles not encountered during training. Planning with Diffusion for Flexible Behavior Synthesis instead represented complete state-action trajectories as a generative solution space, so new rewards, endpoints, or constraints could guide the same learned model. In both cases, learning captures what recurs across tasks while search resolves the route required by the task at hand.

Predictive control repeats this search as the world changes. Flexible Locomotion Learning with Diffusion Model Predictive Control combines a learned trajectory prior with new rewards and constraints, while Model-Based Diffusion Sampling for Predictive Control in Offline Decision Making composes a task planner with action-conditioned dynamics so that task-aligned trajectories remain feasible.

For longer-horizon tasks, video provides an intermediate representation between a high-level goal and low-level control. It can represent what should happen in the scene while leaving an embodiment-specific model to determine how a particular robot makes it happen. Learning Universal Policies via Text-Guided Video Generation learns a task-level visual trajectory that can serve as a proposal from which actions are extracted. Video Language Planning shows how search can compare and connect such proposals into longer-horizon plans. Video therefore exposes a proposed future that search can compare and extend, while allowing different robot embodiments to translate that future into actions.

Different models can also divide the problem across levels of abstraction. Compositional Foundation Models for Hierarchical Planning uses language to propose high-level steps, video to ground them in a scene, and an action model to test their physical executability. Planning becomes a search for a course of action supported at every level. This is how planning grows out of model composition. The components provide complementary views of the same evolving future.

World Action Planner makes the separation between action proposal, consequence prediction, and search explicit. A vision-language model proposes actions, an action-conditioned world model predicts their consequences, and global correction and local search revise the proposal for the current task. The same verification–generation separation that motivates solution inference therefore reappears in sequential decision making. Judging whether an imagined consequence makes progress can be easier than generating the entire successful behavior at once.

For this judgment to guide search, a planning stack must preserve the decision-relevant consequences that change action rankings, including controllability, contact, and object persistence, and expose them so an evaluator can assess progress toward the goal. Perceptual realism alone is not enough.

Planning and policy can therefore occupy different points on a moving amortization boundary. Unfamiliar behavior can initially be constructed through search, while behavior that recurs can later be compiled into a reusable policy or skill.

Once the robot begins executing a plan, however, the observed world may depart from the predicted future. The mug may shift in the robot's grasp or a dish may block the route. The planner can use the resulting state to search again rather than continue an obsolete action sequence. Replanning changes the action sequence, but it may still use the same search procedure each time.

Procedure control

Control the reasoning process

Deferred decisionWhich operation and for how long?

The first three scopes leave an object of inference open—a candidate, a task model, or a course of action—but may still use a fixed outer procedure. Replanning alone does not imply procedure control: a system may simply rerun the same planner. Procedure control begins when evidence from the current computation determines what to preserve, which operation to run next, or how much computation to allocate. It does not require inventing a new algorithm at test time. The outer controller may remain fixed while the operation it selects remains open. A system that always runs the same solver for a preset number of steps infers only the object, not the procedure.

Temporal Backtracking Search for Test-time Generative Video Reasoning makes this distinction concrete for generative video. The video model is trained to resume from an arbitrary valid prefix. After each rollout, a process verifier localizes the first failure, identifies a safe restart point, and ranks the surviving prefixes. The outer generate–verify–restart loop remains fixed, but evidence from the current rollout determines where generation resumes, which prefix is expanded next, and whether search stops. This kind of procedure control is possible because the model exposes the intermediate state that the controller needs to preserve and revise.

The right procedure depends on both what can be recovered from the current solution and how much delay the situation permits. Adaptive Online Replanning with Diffusion Models uses the diffusion model's estimated likelihood of the current plan to decide when to keep executing it and when to replan. Replanning can bootstrap from the existing trajectory to preserve its goal. Win Fast or Lose Slow dynamically selects model size and quantization according to real-time demands, showing that the best procedure depends on the cost of delay as well as answer quality. A controller therefore needs signals about both what can be recovered and how much time the current problem allows.

For the robot, this means that a poor view of the mug or a slight shift of the mug in the robot's grasp need not trigger a complete restart. The controller may rerun perception, resample the grasp, check that the mug is still upright, repair only the affected part of the plan, switch methods, or stop deliberating and continue.

Procedure control assumes that the sources of knowledge available to the system are already fixed. When the current problem must also determine which differentiated generators, verifiers, or specialists participate—and how their outputs are connected—the organization itself becomes an object of inference.

Reasoner organization

Assemble the reasoning system

Deferred decisionWhich reasoners and connections?

Procedure control chooses the next operation within an already assembled system. Reasoner organization leaves the assembly itself open. Inference may determine which specialists and tools participate, what roles they take on, what state is shared, and how information moves among them. This differs from model composition, which constructs the task model that defines what counts as a solution. Reasoner organization instead assembles the specialists that supply or evaluate the relevant information. The two can work together. If the robot encounters an unfamiliar, fragile mug, for example, the system may bring in a grasp specialist and route its judgment to the planner.

Organizational choices can lie at different points on the amortization boundary. Multiagent Debate fixes its participants, roles, and communication protocol in advance. Multi-Agent Verification likewise uses a fixed set of aspect verifiers and a fixed aggregation rule. Multiagent Finetuning instead learns complementary generator and critic behaviors from their interaction, but it too uses a fixed organization at deployment. These approaches show how recurring protocols and roles can be fixed or learned before test time. Inference then operates within the resulting organization rather than deciding how to assemble it for the current problem.

When organization must adapt to the current problem, a reusable coordination mechanism can specify incentives and local rules. These allow reasoners to decide when to participate and how their contributions are valued, so that a task-specific organization emerges without a central scheduler. Economy of Minds follows this pattern. At evaluation, auctions select a problem-dependent sequence of specialists from a fixed population. Across optimization episodes, economic selection reshapes that population. Because selecting who acts also determines what computation runs next, the mechanism spans procedure control and reasoner organization. Its organizational component lies in selecting which specialists contribute to the current trajectory.

Integration test

Why embodiment is central

Robotics provides a natural integration test for generalization by construction because it can bring all five scopes of inference into play while each action changes the state from which the next decision must be made. In the coffee task, an unreachable placement leaves planning with no feasible route, a failed grasp changes the next observation, and persistent uncertainty may require a different procedure or specialist. A robot cannot infer everything from scratch before every action, but it also cannot compile away choices that an unfamiliar goal, constraint, or failure may require it to reconsider. The amortization boundary must be placed across the full system so that action remains both timely and adaptable.

Conclusion

Generalization as construction

Logic- and planning-based symbolic systems preserved a separation between knowledge and inference, but relied on representations built by hand. Neural models made representations learnable from data, but often compiled away flexibility in how that knowledge could be used. The synthesis proposed here is to learn reusable structure while preserving the choices inference may need to revisit as the current problem unfolds.

A problem can be globally unfamiliar even when the structure needed to solve it is locally familiar. If learning captures that structure in reusable form, inference can use it to construct a solution whose exact form was never encountered during training. The five scopes show where this construction can occur—in a candidate solution, the model that defines the problem, a course of action, the sequence of reasoning operations used to solve it, or the organization of reasoners.

The boundary between what is fixed in advance and what is resolved through inference can move. Inference can handle unfamiliar problems when they first arise. If parts of that computation repeat across problems, learning can later absorb them and make them fast. Generalization by construction is not an argument for leaving every decision to inference. It is a design principle. Recurring computation should be amortized without compiling away decisions that an unfamiliar goal, world state, or source of expertise may require the system to revisit. Systems should therefore be judged not only by their speed on familiar tasks, but also by what they can reconfigure through inference when the problem changes.
