Generalizable intelligence

Generalization by Construction

What learning should leave open for inference

August 29, 2026

The ultimate goal of artificial intelligence is to build systems that can act effectively in situations they have never seen before. This is especially important in robotics, where a robot will face new environments, objects, constraints, and goals. The dominant approach, however, is to collect ever-larger datasets and train end-to-end neural networks to map observations directly to behavior. This has produced powerful systems, but it is practically impossible to gather examples covering the open-ended space of possible situations.

One important direction in symbolic AI began with a different premise. It separated knowledge from the procedure used to reason with it. Facts, relations, rules, actions, and goals were represented as explicit objects that could be reused and recombined after a goal was specified. A planner could then pursue a new goal or incorporate a new constraint without training a new model. McCarthy's Programs with Common Sense and the STRIPS planning system are early examples of this approach.

The difficulty was deciding what to represent and how to search over it. People often had to choose the symbols, predicates, and operators, connect perception to them, and decide which details of the world to ignore. Search became difficult in large, continuous, and partially observed spaces. For images, video, language, and physical interaction, even the right vocabulary is difficult to specify in advance.

Deep learning made it possible to learn powerful representations directly from large, high-dimensional datasets. When a network is trained to predict behavior directly, the representations and the procedure for using them are learned together. The resulting mapping can generalize to situations that follow the regularities it has learned. But when a new problem falls outside those regularities, the system may have no explicit way to compare alternatives or revise an intermediate decision.

I argue that a globally unfamiliar problem can often be solved by recombining locally familiar structure. A robot may face a new situation even when the relevant objects, dynamics, and skills are all familiar. By learning models of this reusable structure, we can give the robot the pieces needed to construct a solution after the task is known. Inference then determines how to combine these pieces and searches for a solution that satisfies the current goals and constraints. The central design question is what learning should capture in advance and what it should leave open for inference to construct.

Core claim: Learning captures locally reusable structure. Inference uses it to construct solutions to globally unfamiliar problems.

The thesis

Drawing the amortization boundary

Every system divides problem solving between choices absorbed into reusable computation before the current problem is known and choices resolved through inference for the problem at hand. I call the placement of this division the amortization boundary. Training can amortize choices that recur across problems, including choices whose dependence on the input can be learned reliably. Choices that a future problem may need to reconsider must instead remain open to inference. Here I use inference to mean problem-specific search, selection, or refinement over choices that remain open to revision for the current problem.

The boundary trades computation for flexibility. If a recurring decision remains open even though the current problem provides no new information about it, inference wastes work. If nearly every decision is fixed in advance, a new goal or constraint may have nowhere to enter. The aim is therefore not to maximize inference, but to leave open the choices whose values cannot safely be determined in advance.

The amortization boundary also changes the generalization problem itself. A direct predictor must learn how a problem maps to a complete solution. Solution inference instead uses a learned criterion for judging candidates, while search constructs a solution for the current problem. Once a candidate is proposed, judging it can depend on fewer aspects of the problem and require a simpler computation than producing the solution directly. When this judgment depends on simpler, reusable structure, learning the criterion can support better generalization than learning the full problem-to-solution mapping.

Model composition extends this idea to the criterion itself. A learned factor can judge whether a candidate satisfies one requirement, such as stable support or clearance from nearby objects. Each factor need only represent the relationships relevant to that requirement. By composing these factors, the system can construct a model for a new combination of requirements without learning a separate model for the entire task. I expect this separation to be especially useful when goals and constraints change while the local relationships and dynamics needed to evaluate candidates remain reusable.

Program specialization offers a useful analogy. A programmer can compile known assumptions into an efficient executable, while retaining a higher-level program in which those assumptions can be revised. A direct neural policy resembles an aggressively specialized executable. It is fast to run, but hard to adapt when a new problem violates its compiled assumptions. An energy function or world model can preserve a higher-level description of possible solutions or futures that sampling, optimization, or planning specializes for the current problem.

This separation also lets a robot search at a level of abstraction where fewer decisions need to be resolved. A planner can search over task-relevant states, subgoals, or trajectories while learned skills handle familiar local transitions. The planner can then construct a new course of action without searching over every motor command.

This suggests four practical steps.

  1. Identify a decision whose answer changes across tasks while the structure needed to evaluate alternatives remains reusable.
  2. Choose a level of representation at which that choice can be inspected and revised.
  3. Learn reusable structure that helps resolve the choice without fixing it in advance.
  4. Use inference to resolve it for the current problem.

My PhD thesis and Compositional Generative Modeling developed this approach through the composition of learned energy landscapes. Here I use the amortization boundary to examine how the same principle can shape both the solutions a system constructs and the computation it uses to construct them.

The framework

Hierarchical inference

Consider a household robot asked to bring a mug of coffee from a crowded kitchen counter to someone at the table. It must lift the mug without disturbing the surrounding dishes, carry it without spilling, and set it within easy reach. The robot may have encountered every object and practiced every skill involved without having faced this particular combination.

The robot can construct both a solution to this task and the computation used to find it. I distinguish five scopes by what inference can reconfigure. The first three construct the problem and its solution, while the final two construct the computation used to solve it.

Procedure control decides how the search proceeds, while reasoner organization determines which specialists contribute and how they communicate.

Solution inference

Search a learned solution space

Deferred decisionWhich candidate?

In solution inference, learning provides an evaluator that scores proposed solutions. Given the current problem, a sampler or optimizer uses this evaluator to compare candidates and revise them toward a better solution. The evaluator remains fixed during this search, while the candidate changes.

In the coffee task, the robot can revise a placement that is unstable or difficult for the person to reach. A direct predictor must produce a suitable placement from the kitchen scene. Given a proposed placement, an evaluator can judge familiar properties such as stability and reachability without learning how to produce the placement itself. Search uses these judgments to construct a placement for the current scene.

An energy-based model represents these judgments as a landscape in which preferred candidates have lower energy. In Implicit Generation and Generalization in Energy-Based Models, generation uses Langevin dynamics to search the learned landscape. Each step follows the negative energy gradient while adding random noise. The sampler does not impose a fixed generation order and can start from an external proposal or run for different numbers of steps.

In Learning Iterative Reasoning through Energy Minimization, iterative inference searches a conditional energy landscape for a solution. Trained on ten-node graphs, the model achieves lower error on shortest-path prediction in fifteen-node graphs than the feedforward and iterative baselines tested. Its verifier–generator analysis motivates why evaluation can require a simpler computation than construction.

Search also depends on the shape of the learned landscape. Learning Iterative Reasoning through Energy Diffusion moves through a sequence of landscapes, from broad, smooth basins toward sharper constraints. Reasoning with Sampling searches over sequences scored by a frozen language model. Its iterative sampler targets a sharpened power distribution derived from the model's sequence likelihood and can revisit earlier choices. Search needs both a landscape that supports revision and scores that remain informative about solution quality as it explores new candidates.

Model composition

Compose the model required by the problem

Deferred decisionWhich task model?

A new task may require a combination of goals and constraints that no single learned model represents. Model composition lets the system construct this model after the task is known. Inference selects the relevant factors and combines them into a model that guides the search for a solution. The same factors can therefore be used to define different tasks.

For the coffee task, the robot can combine factors that favor an upright mug, clearance from nearby dishes, stable support on the table, and reachability for the person. A support factor can evaluate the mug and its supporting surface, while a clearance factor evaluates the mug and nearby objects. Neither factor needs to model the entire kitchen arrangement. By composing these local judgments, the robot can evaluate an arrangement it has never seen as a whole. The factors support new combinations when their judgments remain valid in the new context and together capture the relationships needed to evaluate a solution.

Symbolic systems obtained much of their flexibility by composing reusable predicates and operators. Learned factors offer a way to retain this flexibility for images, language, and physical interaction, where the relevant relations are difficult to specify by hand. Composition then provides a way to program a new task using models learned from earlier data. Factors from different architectures or modalities need compatible ways to evaluate or guide a shared candidate, and an inference procedure that reconciles their constraints.

Generalizable Reasoning through Compositional Energy Minimization tests this reuse on Boolean satisfiability. It learns clause-level energies and refines their composition on small training problems. At test time, the same local factors are composed to define larger problems. Training uses instances with at most twenty variables, while evaluation includes fifty-variable instances, where the method solves more problems than the neural baselines tested.

Composition can operate across several kinds of learned structure. Concept distributions can be combined through conjunction, disjunction, and negation, named spatial relations can be assembled into a scene, and independently trained score models can be combined during denoising. Iterative consensus extends the same principle across architectures by letting a generator and independently trained vision, language, or task-specific scorers communicate through a shared candidate.

Learning can also discover new factors to compose. Unsupervised Learning of Compositional Energy Concepts discovers reusable factors without concept labels, while Few-Shot Task Learning through Inverse Generative Modeling infers a new task factor from demonstrations without changing the pretrained generator. By combining a new factor with earlier ones, the system can expand the set of tasks it represents without retraining every component.

The factors have to be grounded in the current problem, and the solver has to match the model they define. Set It Up! uses a symbolic relation graph to select learned continuous factors and ground them into object poses. Reduce, Reuse, Recycle shows that standard reverse diffusion with composed scores does not in general sample the intended product distribution.

For decision making, composition brings together a description of what should happen and a model of what actions can cause. Objectives can combine goals, rewards, and constraints, while dynamics can combine models of objects, agents, and interactions. Action planning uses the resulting model to search for actions whose predicted consequences satisfy the objective.

Action planning

Plan through possible futures

Deferred decisionWhich actions reach the goal?

In the coffee task, the robot must still choose a grasp, lift the mug clear of nearby dishes, carry it without spilling, and place it at the selected location. Planning leaves this action sequence open. The planner predicts the consequences of possible actions and searches for a sequence expected to reach the desired state.

A learned dynamics model predicts what an action will do, leaving the planner to determine which actions serve the current goal. For a new goal under familiar dynamics, the planner can reuse this model and search for a different action sequence. This gives learning a reusable prediction problem while inference constructs the behavior required by the task. Behavior that recurs can later be absorbed into a policy or skill.

A planner can search over subgoals, intermediate states, or complete trajectories. Model-Based Planning with Energy-Based Models inferred intermediate states under learned dynamics, allowing a planner to route around obstacles not encountered during training. Planning with Diffusion for Flexible Behavior Synthesis instead represented complete state-action trajectories as a generative solution space, so new rewards, endpoints, or constraints could guide the same learned model.

Predictive control repeats this search as the world changes. Flexible Locomotion Learning with Diffusion Model Predictive Control combines a learned trajectory prior with new rewards and constraints, while Model-Based Diffusion Sampling for Predictive Control in Offline Decision Making composes a task planner with action-conditioned dynamics to improve the feasibility of task-aligned trajectories.

For longer-horizon tasks, video can represent how a scene should change while leaving an embodiment-specific model to determine the robot's actions. Learning Universal Policies via Text-Guided Video Generation uses a task-level visual trajectory as a proposal from which actions are extracted. Video Language Planning searches over these visual proposals to construct longer-horizon plans.

Planning can also bring models at different levels of abstraction into the same search. Compositional Foundation Models for Hierarchical Planning uses language to propose high-level steps, video to ground them in a scene, and an action model to test their physical executability. Together, these models let the planner search for a course of action consistent with the goal, the scene, and the robot's capabilities.

In World Action Planner, a vision-language model proposes actions and an action-conditioned world model predicts their consequences. Global correction and local search then revise the proposal for the current task. Judging whether an imagined consequence makes progress can be easier than generating the entire successful behavior at once, giving planning the same potential advantage as solution inference.

Procedure control

Control the reasoning process

Deferred decisionWhich operation and for how long?

The computation used to find a solution can itself be constructed from reusable operations. A system may have useful generators, verifiers, and search procedures without knowing in advance which sequence of operations will solve a new problem. Procedure control uses evidence from the computation so far to decide what to preserve, which operation to run next, and how much computation to spend. The controller and total budget can remain fixed even as these choices change with the problem.

Temporal Backtracking Search for Test-time Generative Video Reasoning trains a video model to resume generation from an arbitrary valid prefix. After each rollout, a process verifier localizes the first failure, identifies a safe restart point, and ranks the surviving prefixes. The outer generate–verify–restart loop remains fixed, but evidence from the current rollout determines where generation resumes, which prefix is expanded next, and whether search stops.

On Keys-and-Doors puzzles with solution paths longer than those in training, this prefix reuse improves success over sampling complete rollouts at the same generation budget. Symbolic verification identifies which parts of the generated trajectory can be retained, and every solved episode in this held-out setting comes from a restarted branch. By retaining these prefixes, inference can solve puzzles that complete rollouts rarely handle.

The right procedure depends on both what can be recovered from the current solution and how much delay the situation permits. Adaptive Online Replanning with Diffusion Models uses the diffusion model's estimated likelihood of the current plan to decide when to keep executing it and when to replan. It can begin replanning from the existing trajectory to preserve the goal. Win Fast or Lose Slow adjusts model size and quantization to balance answer quality against delay.

If the robot has a poor view of the mug, the controller may rerun perception. If the mug shifts in the robot's grasp, the controller may check that the mug is still upright, resample the grasp, or repair the affected part of the plan. The controller can make these local repairs without restarting the whole computation, or stop deliberating when it has enough information to act.

Reasoner organization

Assemble the reasoning system

Deferred decisionWhich reasoners and connections?

Constructing a reasoning procedure can also require choosing who participates in it. If the robot encounters an unfamiliar, fragile mug, it may need to consult a grasping specialist and pass that judgment to the planner. Different specialists can learn from different sets of problems, while the current task determines which of their abilities must be brought together. Reasoner organization leaves this choice of participants open to inference, along with their roles, shared state, and connections.

Multiagent Debate and Multi-Agent Verification establish their participants and coordination rules before deployment. Multiagent Finetuning learns complementary generator and critic behaviors, also within a fixed organization at deployment.

Economy of Minds uses incentives and local rules to coordinate specialists without a central scheduler. At evaluation, auctions select a problem-dependent sequence of specialists from a fixed population. Across optimization episodes, economic selection reshapes that population. Selecting who acts also determines what computation runs next, so this mechanism spans procedure control and reasoner organization.

I expect this ability to reorganize specialists to support generalization to tasks they were never trained to solve together. Each specialist can contribute a familiar capability even when the full task requires an unfamiliar combination. A useful test is to hold the specialists and computation budget fixed, then compare a task-dependent organization with a fixed one on new task families.

Integration test

Why embodiment is central

A robot has to make these different forms of inference work together while it acts. In the coffee task, a placement may be stable and within the person's reach yet impossible for the robot to reach safely. Planning must then reopen the placement choice. A failed grasp may require a new trajectory, while persistent uncertainty may call for a different procedure or specialist. Evidence from one scope can require the system to revise a decision made at another. These revisions must happen in time for the robot to act.

Conclusion

Generalization as construction

A problem can be globally unfamiliar even when the structure needed to solve it is locally familiar. Judging a candidate can be simpler than producing the whole solution, and local factors can remain useful across new combinations of goals and constraints. Learning can capture these reusable judgments while inference constructs the solution required by the current problem.

I expect general-purpose agents to build on learned models, skills, and specialists while constructing the plans and reasoning procedures required by each new task. As a solution recurs, learning can absorb the repeated parts of its construction. The amortization boundary can therefore move with experience. Learning should make familiar tasks faster to solve while preserving the ability to construct new solutions when the goal, the world, or the available expertise changes.
